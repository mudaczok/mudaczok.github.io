Over the summer, MyDr, a service widely used by doctors to manage patient records and medical documentation, was hacked.


[Official statement from the Polish Ministry of Health](https://www.gov.pl/web/zdrowie/informacja-w-zwiazku-z-incydentem-dotyczacym-firmy-mydr)

Just a week ago, news broke that Autopay had also suffered a successful cyberattack. Even the company itself isn't entirely sure whether customers' payment card details were compromised.


[Read more](https://cyfrowa.rp.pl/bezpieczenstwo/art45487531-hakerzy-zaatakowali-autopay-firma-ostrzega-klientow-przed-zagrozeniem)

And today, we've learned that 1Password has once again proven to have security vulnerabilities.


[Official update from 1Password](https://1password.com/blog/update-on-nov-2026-security-incident)

That said, I honestly don't understand what kind of idiot you'd have to be to store your passwords somewhere in the cloud, even if they're supposedly protected by some kind of extraterrestrial encryption technology.

Anyway, I've come to believe that the moment a service gets hold of my data — whether it's completely made up or, God forbid, real because the nature of the platform requires it — that data should be considered compromised by default.

That's why, over the past few years, I've shifted my focus away from blindly trusting the promises made by shady companies and massive corporations. Instead, I've focused on making sure that some hypothetical North Korean or Russian hacker can't get their hands on my money.

Cash is safe, reliable, and incredibly convenient.

As for my passport number, home address, or pictures of my dick that I've sent to my girlfriend — none of that really matters anymore.

Anyone who still blindly puts their faith in compliance regulations, encryption, the judicial system, and personal data protection laws is going to be the one crying the loudest when things eventually go south.
